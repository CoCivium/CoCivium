# Facilitator Guide — Watershed Explorer R0

## Objective

Help learners build and revise a causal explanation of local stormwater movement while preserving a strict distinction between:

- model;
- observation;
- inference;
- evidence;
- uncertainty.

The facilitator is not expected to be a hydrologist.

## Materials

Minimum:

- one shallow tray or equivalent digital model;
- absorbent material such as sponge/felt/soil substitute;
- impermeable material such as foil/plastic;
- marked measuring cup, syringe, bottle, or another simple way to use the same nominal water volume;
- timer or stopwatch;
- two collection points/cups;
- learner sheets;
- **dated printed/PDF/local source extracts available without internet access**.

Optional:

- topographic/watershed map;
- tablet;
- local rain gauge;
- turbidity/temperature/conductivity sensor;
- shared display;
- VR/MR visualization.

## Safety

For physical water models:

- use small quantities of clean water;
- keep water away from powered electronics;
- wipe spills immediately;
- do not use unknown creek/pond water in the classroom model;
- do not ask learners to sample real water without a separately qualified field protocol.

## Before learners arrive

1. Build two simple surfaces:
   - more absorbent/permeable;
   - more hard/impermeable.
2. Make sure both drain toward a visible collection point.
3. Choose a nominal water volume and pour interval and write them down.
4. Test the same nominal volume/rate on both surfaces. Record any obvious procedural difference.
5. Place learner materials.
6. Prepare dated local source extracts so the source challenge works if the internet is unavailable.
7. If using immersive content, verify the non-headset path works first.

## Phase 1 — Hook, about 8 minutes

Ask:

> When rain lands on this building, parking lot, field, or street, where can the water go next?

Do not correct immediately.

Capture candidate pathways such as:

- infiltrates into ground;
- evaporates;
- runs over land;
- enters catch basin;
- enters storm sewer;
- reaches pond;
- reaches creek;
- reaches Lake Ontario.

Mark uncertain relations with a question mark.

## Phase 2 — Build the model, about 15 minutes

Teams inspect the two surfaces.

Ask them to identify which features are meant to represent:

- hard surface;
- permeable ground;
- slope;
- drain;
- pond/creek outlet.

Then ask:

> What does this model leave out?

Useful answers include vegetation, soil type, pipe capacity, slope detail, rainfall duration, snowmelt, temperature, pollutants, maintenance, groundwater and changing weather.

Do not reward a longer list merely for being longer. Reward relevance.

## Phase 3 — Predict, about 10 minutes

Each team writes one pre-test prediction.

Example structure:

> If we increase the hard-surface area while keeping the poured water amount similar, then ______ will change because ______.

Require a directional prediction if possible.

The prediction must be written before the test.

PREDICTION_AFTER_OUTCOME_NE_PREDICTION

## Phase 4 — Test and observe, about 15 minutes

Use the same **nominal measured volume** and approximately the same pour interval for each surface. Use a timer where practical.

The goal is simple repeatability, not laboratory-grade hydrology. If the procedure differs between trials, record that difference instead of pretending it did not happen.

Learners record:

- visible runoff;
- time to outlet;
- approximate collected volume;
- pooling;
- infiltration/absorption;
- unexpected effects.

Ask learners to label every note as one of:

- **MODEL_OBSERVATION** — directly noticed in the tray/digital simulation;
- **WORLD_OBSERVATION** — directly measured or seen in the real environment, if a separately qualified field activity exists;
- **SOURCE_STATEMENT** — reported by a cited external source;
- **INFERENCE** — a conclusion drawn from one or more observations/statements.

Example:

"Water reached cup B first in our tray" = MODEL_OBSERVATION.

"The hard surface causes more flooding in Oakville" = INFERENCE that exceeds this model.

**MODEL_OBSERVATION_NE_WORLD_OBSERVATION**

## Phase 5 — Source challenge, about 10 minutes

Use current official source material.

Relevant local facts include:

- Oakville describes stormwater as rain/snowmelt runoff from roofs, streets and other surfaces.
- Oakville's system includes storm pipes, catch basins, ponds, creeks, shoreline and ditches.
- Hard surfaces can increase fast runoff because water cannot easily absorb into the ground.
- Stormwater ponds are used to help manage runoff quantity and quality.
- Conservation Halton offers watershed/water-quality field education.

Ask:

1. Which part of our model is supported by these sources?
2. Which part is not tested by these sources?
3. What new relation should we add?
4. Which statement needs to become more cautious?

## Phase 6 — Revise explanation, about 10 minutes

Learners rewrite their explanation using:

> We first thought ______.

> We observed ______.

> The source evidence adds ______.

> We now think ______, with confidence ______ because ______.

> We are still uncertain about ______.

Require at least one uncertainty.

## Phase 7 — Choose next measurement, about 7 minutes

Ask:

> If we could measure one real thing outside to reduce uncertainty, what should it be?

Candidate measurements:

- rainfall amount/rate;
- surface type/area;
- slope;
- flow speed;
- creek level;
- water temperature;
- turbidity;
- time between rain and creek rise.

The best answer is not predetermined. It should be linked to the team's uncertainty.


## 45-minute minimum path

If the session loses time, preserve the evidence loop and remove optional enrichment first.

Minimum sequence:

1. Hook / relation sketch — 5 min
2. Prediction — 7 min
3. Controlled model test — 10 min
4. Observation classification — 6 min
5. Source challenge — 7 min
6. Revised explanation + uncertainty — 7 min
7. Next measurement — 3 min

Drop VR/MR, extended discussion, repeated trials and optional visualization **before** dropping the precommitted prediction, source challenge, revision or uncertainty.

## Optional immersive extension

If VR/MR is available, visualize:

- rain falling over different surfaces;
- flow entering catch basins;
- pipe/pond/creek relations;
- a transparent view of alternative runoff pathways.

The immersive scene must label:

- simulated;
- simplified;
- not to scale where applicable;
- source date;
- missing processes.

Do not present a photorealistic simulation as measured reality.

If a learner reports dizziness, nausea, disorientation, pain, panic or other discomfort, stop immersive participation immediately and continue through the non-headset path without penalty.

**SAFETY_STOP_OVERRIDES_LESSON_COMPLETION**

## Optional destination/field extension

A later visit may connect to:

- a visible stormwater-management feature;
- a creek observation point where lawful/safe;
- a Conservation Halton education program.

The field extension requires its own permissions, safety and supervision plan.

## Debrief prompts

- What did the model make easy to see?
- What did the model hide?
- What observation changed your mind?
- What source changed your confidence?
- What would you measure next?
- What claim would you refuse to make from this evidence?

## Facilitator quality check

PASS if learners can distinguish:

- what they saw;
- what they inferred;
- what the model suggested;
- what an external source supports;
- what remains unknown.

FAIL if the lesson ends with only "impermeable surfaces are bad" or another slogan without causal/evidence reasoning.

Also FAIL if:
- a tray result is reported as a real Oakville measurement;
- internet loss prevents the source challenge;
- a learner who cannot use VR receives an inferior learning task;
- safety/comfort is subordinated to completing the immersive experience.
