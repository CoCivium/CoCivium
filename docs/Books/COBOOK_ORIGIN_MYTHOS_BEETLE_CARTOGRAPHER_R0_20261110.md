## An origin scene

### The Beetle and the Unfinished Map

In a city full of doors, nearly every door came with instructions.

Walk straight. Think straight. Keep your questions until the end. A few rules kept everyone safe. Others had survived so long that nobody remembered what danger they were meant to prevent.

The Beetle had spent years inspecting doors. Its antennae were forever getting caught in the hinges.

Sometimes the Beetle had been right. Sometimes it had been insufferable. On its better days, it knew the difference. Doors rarely appreciated that distinction.

It earned its living fixing a hinge here, drawing a new doorway there, and leaving before its questions filled the room. It was useful enough to invite back but inconvenient enough to keep out of the official portraits.

Then the Cartographer came along.

The Cartographer was young, but not an apprentice beetle. It noticed different things, wanted different destinations, and refused to call a straight line the only possible route.

“Why do you keep working on doors you don't want to walk through?” the Cartographer asked.

The Beetle took a while to answer.

“I have a rather selfish reason. I want you to inherit more choices than I did.”

“That's your reason,” said the Cartographer. “What if I like one of those doors?”

“Then keep it.”

“And what if I want to make something that isn't a door?”

“Then I may need you to teach me what to call it.”

They worked on a map, though neither agreed where its center should be.

When the Beetle marked the place it had once fallen, the Cartographer drew a bridge beside it. When the Cartographer drew a path the Beetle did not understand, the Beetle left it uncorrected.

A whale looked at the map and asked whether anyone had remembered to draw the sea. A mouse pointed out the gaps where small travelers could pass. A machine offered a neat answer, then changed it when the others supplied better evidence. The map grew less impressive and more useful.

“Who gets to own it?” the Cartographer asked.

“The people who can use it, challenge it, and leave it better,” the Beetle said. “Though they should know whose drawing each line came from.”

“Will you lead?”

“I could sit in the back and tell you where I once got lost.”

“Maybe,” said the Cartographer. “But some days I won't want directions.”

The Beetle nodded. The Cartographer rolled the map open. There were more blank spaces than roads.

The Beetle never became a dragon. It stayed small, which was convenient for finding the cracks where a door could begin to open.

And that is why the story begins not with a crown, but with a beetle and an unfinished map.

## Why this belongs in the Field Guide

The apparent paradox is the motive: a character who argues with institutions and gives away useful designs does not have to be either a saint or an omniscient rebel. The Beetle has a personal, ordinary stake in the work: it loves a younger person and hopes they inherit more choices. **Love is a motive, not proof that every judgment is right.** The Cartographer is a co-protagonist with an independent future, not a vessel for the Beetle's ideals.

The conflict is not that all institutions are villains or that unusual minds are automatically superior. The testable questions are whether procedures fit real users, whether disagreements can improve a shared map, and whether collaborators retain freedom to choose another route.
